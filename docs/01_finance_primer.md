# Finance Primer

## Interest rates

An interest rate is the price of borrowing money. If a bank, company, or
government borrows today, the interest rate says how much extra it must pay back
later. Interest rates also matter to savers because they are the return earned
for lending money or holding interest-paying assets.

## Treasuries

Debt securities issued by the government. When an investor buys a Treasury, 
they are lending money to the government. Treasuries are widely watched because 
they are considered very low credit risk and are usedas reference rates across 
finance.

## Maturity

Maturity is the time until a debt security is repaid. A 3-month Treasury matures
soon, while a 30-year Treasury matures far in the future. Longer maturities
usually involve more uncertainty about inflation, economic growth, and future
interest rates.

## Yield curves

A yield curve plots Treasury yields across maturities on one date. The short end
shows yields such as 3 months or 2 years. The long end shows yields such as 10
or 30 years. A normal upward-sloping curve means longer maturities have higher
yields than shorter maturities.

## Basis points

A basis point is one hundredth of one percentage point. For example, 25 basis
points equals 0.25 percentage points. Bond markets use basis points because many
daily yield moves are small.

## Time value of money

The time value of money means a dollar today is usually worth more than a dollar
in the future. Money today can be invested, and future money is uncertain. Yields
are one way markets translate future payments into values today.

## Yield-curve inversion

A yield-curve inversion happens when a shorter maturity yield is higher than a
longer maturity yield. A common example is the 2-year yield above the 10-year
yield. This can happen when markets expect future interest rates or growth to
fall. Inversions are watched closely because they have often appeared before
economic slowdowns, but they are not perfect timing tools.
